Working title · ISS · Co-op first-person horror

It has no eyes. It hunts by sound.

Six people wake alone and scattered aboard a dead International Space Station. No gravity. No lights. Something aboard that hears everything. You have to find each other, bring four systems back online and reach the capsule — and every single thing you do is a decision about how loud to be.

The pitch

Every other co-op horror game is about not being seen.

That is a solved problem. Players learn the sight-lines, crouch in the dark and stop being afraid.

ISS takes sight off the table entirely. The alien has no vision cone — not a hard one, not a soft one, none — and we tell the player that on the main menu. It knows only what it hears. So the map is not a set of hiding places; it's an acoustic graph. Sound spreads module to module, loses a fixed amount per metre, and loses more at every hatch it has to get through. A closed hatch is worth 25 points of silence. A sealed one is worth 40.

Which means the thing players are actually managing is a budget. Sliding along a handrail costs 4. Cycling a hatch costs 45. Prying a jammed panel costs 60 and takes three seconds instead of twenty-five. And your own voice — real proximity voice chat, sampled from your real microphone — costs anywhere from 10 to 55 depending on how badly you need to be heard.

Nobody has to be told to whisper. They just start whispering.

Three pillars · everything else gets cut

What the whole game is made of

If a feature doesn't serve one of these, it doesn't ship. That rule has already removed a rollback netcode layer, a server-side physics engine, a sanity meter and a stamina bar.

Pillar 01

Sound is the only currency

Movement, puzzles, voice chat, even the proximity tracker on your wrist — every one is a noise decision with a printed price. The player must always be able to answer one question: was that loud?

Pillar 02

Zero-G is the risk system

Nobody walks. You haul along handrails — slow and quiet — or push off and fly, and you'd better catch something at the far end. Traversal is already a gamble, so we don't need a second meter to make it tense.

Pillar 03

Legible, not realistic

A player should build a correct mental model of the alien's hearing inside one round. Sound falls off linearly, not by inverse-square, because realism here makes the mechanic unreadable. Fairness is what makes it frightening instead of infuriating.

Movement

Three ways across a room, three ways to die

You can slide along the rails at 1.2 m/s and stay almost silent. You can charge a push-off and fly across at up to 6 m/s. Or you can miss.

The critical detail — the one that turns this from a movement system into a skill ceiling — is that a clean catch and a crash are two different sounds. Catching a handrail at full speed costs 26. Hitting a bulkhead at full speed costs 51. Skill buys you roughly a module and a half of silence, every single time you cross a room, and that is what makes going fast a real choice instead of a mistake.

A chase in a five-metre cylinder has no flanking, so we designed the loop explicitly: it closes at 3.0 → you push off and gain ground → you must catch (26, it keeps its fix on you) or crash (51, it definitely does) → you close a hatch behind you for 45, which buys three seconds, and it opens that hatch for 45 too, so you hear it coming.

The antagonist

A monster you can be right about

It moves the way you move — pulling itself along the same handrails, through the same hatches. It perceives sound and physical contact. That is the complete list.

Dormant→ Patrol→ Investigate→ Search→ Hunt→ Attack
Fairness

It never knows exactly where you are

Every noise it hears resolves to a point offset by up to 12 metres, tightening as the noise gets louder — and never, ever below 2 metres. A pry bar next door pins you to about three metres. A whisper across the station buys you twelve. The floor is the guarantee: it cannot teleport onto your exact position.

Attention

It hears better when it's already looking

On patrol it ignores anything arriving under 12. Once it's searching, that drops to 4 — so a rail pull, the quietest thing you own, is completely safe at distance and fatal when it's sweeping your module and you shift your grip. One sentence to explain, and it is the whole tension of the game.

Honesty

It is loud when it charges

A silent charge reads as a bug and feels like cheating, so hunting makes noise, opening a hatch makes noise, and we state the rules on the menu. A legible monster is scarier than a mysterious one, because when it kills you, you know it was your fault.

Two anti-degenerate systems sit underneath: a rolling one-second window keeps it from thrashing between six players' noises, and a repeat penalty means it stops falling for the same decoy in the same place twice. If it has been near a living player for a fuzzed 60–150 seconds without a kill, it leaves — long enough that camping is a gamble, not a proof.

The round

Scatter, reunite, split, converge

Everyone wakes in a different module on emergency lighting with no idea where anybody else is. The only tool you have for finding your crew is your voice — and your voice is the loudest thing you own. Shouting "where is everyone" works, and it is exactly as stupid as it sounds.

Minute 0–4

Scatter

Alone, in the dark. Players tap handrails — a knock costs 15 and carries two modules — and invent their own codes for here, safe, run. We did not design that protocol. They do, every round.

Minute 4–8

Reunite

The most reliably terrifying stretch of the game, and it costs almost nothing to build: random spawns, one hop apart minimum, and a monster that starts at least three hops from the crowd.

Minute 8–18

Split

Four puzzles run in parallel, so six players break into groups — and every group is generating noise that endangers the others. Bringing a system online pushes the station one stage more dangerous.

Minute 18–25

Converge

Three players in three modules hold three release levers for five seconds, counting each other down over voice at loudness 60. The alien is coming and everybody knows it.

The station gets more dangerous as you fix it

Without pressure, the optimal play in a game like this is to move slowly and silently forever, and a group of friends finds that out by round three. So progress itself is the escalation: every system you bring online makes the thing hunting you faster and sharper, and a stalling team escalates anyway on an eight-minute timer. That caps a round at 20–25 minutes and gives every run an arc.

Objectives

The puzzles are easy. Doing them is not.

Six of them, deliberately simple. Nothing here should make a player feel stuck — it should make them feel exposed. And every one has a loud-fast path and a quiet-slow path, which is the rule that keeps the noise system relevant long after the map is learned.

The thesis puzzle · 2 players · 2 modules

Coolant valve

One module has a pressure gauge and no valve. Another has the valve wheel and no gauge. One player turns; the other reads the needle and talks them into the green band. Turning gently costs 8. Spinning the wheel costs 40. So the reader has to stay patient while their partner sits alone in the dark being told to keep going.

Thirty lines of code. It is the entire design in one interaction.

Teaches everything · 1 player

Breaker sequence

Six breakers in the right order. Each throw is a CLACK at 35; a wrong order resets the panel with a buzz at 50. The order is written on a card stowed in a locker somewhere else on the station. Or there's a manual override under the panel — hold a lever for twenty seconds at loudness 6, anchored to a rail, unable to look around.

Commitment · 2 players

Airlock keyswitch

Two keyswitches four metres apart, turned within a second of each other. Trivial on the ground. In zero-G, both players have to anchor to rails and count down over voice — and it is loud at 45 no matter how carefully you do it.

Pacing · any number

Fuse hunt

Three blown fuses, three replacements in randomised lockers, zero logic. Its entire job is to force people to travel while the alien is awake. It is what spaces out the thinky ones.

Two players who work out knock codes can solve the coolant valve through a bulkhead in near silence. We know. We left it in — the group that discovers that will tell the story for years.

Six players

Somebody dies in the first four minutes. Every round.

At six players that is not a risk, it is a certainty, and a friend watching a black screen for twenty minutes is how a group stops playing a game. So attrition is designed, not patched.

01

The dead get a job

Spectators speak to the living over a headset channel at loudness 5 — the team's eyes for almost no noise cost. It keeps the group inside the game's own voice channel, which is the whole ballgame.

02

Revival is a destination

Carry a medkit to the body, not the body to medical. You keep all the tension — loud travel, exposed anchoring, a downed friend as a fixed point on the map — for a fraction of the work.

03

Escaping with three of six is a win

Said out loud on the results screen, and ranked: crew recovered 3/6. A binary win condition at six players means most rounds end in failure, and the group quietly stops showing up.

The known threat, stated plainly

Your friends will be in a Discord call before the game launches, and that defeats voice-as-noise, the reunion phase, knock codes and the coolant valve's entire premise. It cannot be detected and cannot be engineered around. What we do instead: state the contract on the menu the same way we state the alien's rules, and give the dead a legitimate in-game reason to keep talking. It is a social problem, and it is the single biggest threat to this being the game we designed.

Status · this is not a concept document

Eight of nine milestones are playable

Everything below is greyboxed — boxes, cylinders and capsules — and wired end to end against a live server. The station is nine authored modules. The audio is fully graph-resolved per listener.

What could kill this

The honest list

Zero-G is sublime or nauseating, with very little in between

Which is exactly why it was milestone zero and why comfort options — roll-lock, snap-turn, FOV slider, a vignette that tightens with angular velocity — shipped before anything else. It feels good. But motion sickness costs players, permanently, and no amount of design fixes it after the fact.

Discord defeats the central mechanic

Social, not technical, and therefore the one we cannot solve with code. Mitigated by contract and by giving the dead a reason to stay in-channel. Needs to be watched in the first real six-player test, not after.

Six people is a crowd, and crowds are boring

The actual failure mode at six players is not bandwidth — it's six people bunched around one panel while nothing happens. Parallel puzzles have to be in before the first six-player test, or that test tells us nothing.

Silent-slow play dominating

If quiet is always optimal, the game becomes a forty-five-minute stealth chore. The escalation director exists solely to prevent this. If playtests still drag, we escalate harder before we add a single piece of content.

Where it goes next

The systems are proven. Now it needs a room full of friends.

The engineering risk is behind us. What's left is the two things you cannot desk-check: whether six real people in one real session play it the way the model says they will, and whether the art pass makes a capsule into a monster.

Immediate priorities, in order: a full six-player playtest over real network conditions with TURN configured, because one friend behind a bad NAT costs an entire evening; the cargo bag physics puzzle, the designated cut if the schedule turns; spectator module cameras, so the dead have something to look at as well as something to say; and then M8 — animation, art, and a real sound designer, which is where this stops being a prototype and starts being a game.

Sound is the only currency. Everything else was negotiable.
